# Product vision

Learning serves the learner's life. The product should help people venture into the world and use their language skills as best they can, with greater freedom and capability as they grow.

## A familiar place to return to

The experience should feel open. There is always room to ask, “What would you like to do?” The learner brings their own interests, experiences, and reasons for learning; the app welcomes that direction rather than confining them to an all-encompassing learning platform. They arrive intending to learn and can expect exercises and deliberate practice.

There are two equally welcome ways to return. A learner might have fifteen minutes and want to get straight into their routine, without a conversation first. Or they might want to pursue something they care about through Chinese: discussing the TV show they are watching, sharing an experience, or making sense of a difficulty.

A learner might bring a 小红书 post and comment thread whose subject fascinates them but whose intricacies are hard to follow. The app helps them stretch toward it, or offers a more digestible way into the same themes. Their interest remains the destination even when the next step needs to be smaller. The app is both a place to train and a place to pursue those interests.

That openness has continuity behind it. The app remembers what the learner has worked on and helps important takeaways take root over time. Exploration can accumulate into lasting capability, with progress and reinforcement supporting the learner's purposes.

## A trainer and a gym

A useful analogy is a personal trainer together with a gym. Through conversation, potentially mixing languages, the trainer learns the learner's goals and current level, then prescribes and adapts a manageable regimen using carefully designed training equipment.

The learner can say how an exercise felt, what was confusing, or what they want to be able to do. They should not have to diagnose the pedagogy or design their own learning system. The learner owns their interests and experience; the app takes responsibility for the structure, dose, and adjustment of practice.

“Natural” has two meanings here: doing the appropriate mental work within an exercise, and fitting learning into the learner's interests and life. Deliberately artificial drills can serve both. The design question is what practice helps this person develop the skill, without a fixed allegiance to immersion or a prescribed curriculum.

Vocabulary and flashcard practice are the equipment built so far. Reading, writing, speaking, and pronunciation suggest other possible equipment; they are possibilities within the vision, not a selected roadmap.

## Practice that engages the skill

Start by identifying the core instinct a skill depends on. Design practice that engages it, then keep refining the exercise by attending to what the learner is actually doing. A correct answer can conceal a shortcut that trains the wrong association.

The project began as a simple Hanzi-recognition experiment built through vibe coding. Later work on production made the design problem clearer: lossy English glosses and card-specific puzzle solving can reward success at the card while missing the intended language skill. Production is the first personal proof of this iterative design process, not proof of transfer beyond the exercise or a general method for mastery.

The starting conviction is that Hanzi recognition is foundational to literate Chinese. Flashcards are the current method for developing it, with room for other methods.

The aim is a richer relationship with the world through language. Continued dependence on the app is not the ultimate measure of success.
